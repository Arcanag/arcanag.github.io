A win reported without its quality debt isn’t a measurement. It’s marketing.

04 / Metrics under pressure

The rate fell. The bot didn’t.

The client escalated a fall in promise-to-pay on a flagship LLM flow. Instead of defending the bot, I split the funnel stage by stage to find what had actually changed.

5.03→3.03%
PTP rate, the escalated decline
17→30%
voicemail share of “engaged” calls

Voicemail inflated the denominator, so every downstream rate fell without the bot changing.

4.37→3.51
dial attempts per lead

A configuration change, not a performance change.

Replayed on a timeline, the “decline” broke down into explainable parts, each with an owner and a fix. The escalation closed because the client could follow every step in their own raw data.

05 / Testing a strategy

A 1.7% slice. Four wrong assumptions.

The client proposed a dialling strategy built on a chart of 1.7% of attempts. I tested it against the full population of 63M monthly attempts.

  1. 01

    “3–4 PM is the best window.”

    False. The chart’s worst weekday hour, 5 PM, was the population’s best, at 7.62% yield.

  2. 02

    “Time of day matters a lot.”

    The whole weekday curve spans only 0.9–1.6 pp.

  3. 03

    “Saturday and Monday are interchangeable.”

    Monday yields 7.37% against Saturday’s 5.85%, so a Monday attempt is worth 1.26×.

  4. 04

    “Capacity is the constraint.”

    The system had already carried 2.2× its mean daily attempts at peak.

06 / Scale and languages

Eight languages to twelve. A supply chain.

Adding a language to a voicebot isn’t translation. I ran it end to end: an external annotation vendor, tooling, and per-language test dates committed to the client and tracked like any other delivery.

  • 8 original
  • 4 added · all 12 live

Root cause

~46% of engaged calls returned “unknown”

One region’s collections lagged, and the standing explanation was customer behaviour. The speech pipeline was the real cause, so a market complaint became an engineering target with a 17-point language QA checklist.

Corpus audit

All 110 bots and 22,614 prompts reviewed

It found speech markup too faint to hear on ~19,000 prompts and ~800 prompts at risk of half-speed audio. Fixes were phased in.

10 / Compliance as a gate

Two exposures. Caught before build.

Both were found in requirements review, before a single call was made, and fixed the same day. Requirement gaps now block development until resolved.

Exposure 1

Offer details disclosed to third parties

A script set would have read a customer’s offer to other contacts, which the client’s own requirements forbid.

Exposure 2

A promotional call labelled as service

Under telecom regulation, that distinction decides whether the call is allowed at all.

Same weekOwned vendor-risk audit readiness for two banking clients as the assessed vendor, including 46 checkpoint rows across legal, delivery, HR, finance, customer success and IT.

11 / What went wrong

My mistakes. What they changed.

Each failure became a written rule, not a promise to be more careful.

  1. 01

    A client report with ~10 of 13 items wrong

    Status was inferred from who emailed last, not the tracker. Corrected line by line with sources; inferred status is now banned from reporting.

  2. 02

    I called it “scraper abuse”. It was our own load testing.

    The top caller was unflagged internal testing, billed to clients. One adversarial re-read overturned my conclusion, and that re-read is now mandatory before a surprising finding ships.

  3. 03

    Ops debt grew for months unmeasured

    The 29.5% time sink was in the data all along. A weekly hours-per-client KPI now exists so the next drift can’t hide.

  4. 04

    Our tools lost to our inboxes

    Governance that adds friction gets routed around. The rebuild held only because intake became easier than the workaround.

  • ~3 correct, of 13 in the report

12 / In hindsight

Four things. Done differently.

  1. 01

    Instrument before migrating

    The LLM cutovers came before the measurement programme, so the baseline was rebuilt afterwards. The right order is baseline → migrate → audit.

  2. 02

    Ban inferred values on day one

    The ground-truth-first rule was obvious. It wasn’t written down until a bad report forced it.

  3. 03

    Treat judge disagreement as a deliverable

    Withholding the LLM-bot number was right, but “we don’t know yet” arrived late. Judge-agreement checks now run before the audit.

  4. 04

    Start language QA at launch

    The 46% “unknown” rate should have been a launch gate for every language, not a discovery after a market lagged.

Depth taught me how systems fail. Scale is teaching me which failures repeat.

The only durable answer to both is measurement the client could audit.
