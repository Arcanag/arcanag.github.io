01 / The question

Meaning is real. But not invented.

Does meaning exist — or is it a story we tell to avoid a silent, indifferent universe? Most defences retreat into religion or emotion, or collapse under logic. This one grounds meaning in the structure of reality itself.

The usual instinct is to say we create meaning. Sartre said existence precedes essence: we arrive without purpose and invent one. That is partly right, but the nihilist absorbs it easily — yes, you invented a story. So what?

The stronger claim: meaning is not invented. It is discovered, through engagement with a reality that already has structure.

Before anyone finds a rock, it has hardness, weight and shape. A rock hard enough and shaped right affords hammering. That affordance isn’t assigned by a mind; it lives in the relationship between the rock and its world. Picking it up as a hammer doesn’t create meaning — it reads a meaning already latent. The word “hammer” is a cognitive post-it note: useful, but not the meaning itself.

This philosophy wasn’t written in a study. It was built in real time — through argument, contradiction and correction. What follows is what survived.

Meaning lives in causal structure. Labels are how minds index it.

02 / The iterative framework

Start anywhere. Correct everything.

Meaning isn’t always visible. It is uncovered by a loop — and there is no requirement to begin correctly.

  1. Observe
  2. Hypothesise
  3. Test
  4. Correct
  5. Redefine
  6. Repeat until stable

A sharp stone is first called a knife. With refinement it becomes a blade, a chef’s knife, a sword. Each definition is valid in its context and superseded as iteration continues. The definition at any stage isn’t final — but it isn’t arbitrary either. It is the most accurate reading of reality available at that point.

Crucially, logic itself is not a fixed boundary. When every iteration inside a logical framework fails, the framework itself must be challenged, adjusted, and the loop restarted.

Key moveLogic is a navigational tool, not a prison.

03 / Absolute and relative meaning

Labels change. Blueprints don’t.

Every thing carries two kinds of meaning. Culture changes the label; nature defines the blueprint.

Relative meaning

The name and significance a culture assigns

A hammer in one culture, a mace in another, a ritual object in a third.

Absolute meaning

The functional essence that persists

Whatever you call it, a hammer doesn’t become scissors. A weighted striking tool stays a weighted striking tool — no mind assigns that.

04 / Consciousness

A spectrum, not a switch.

Consciousness isn’t a ghost in the machine. It is what sufficiently complex logical pathways feel like from the inside — and it differs by degree, not kind.

  • Slime moulds
  • Insects
  • Mammals
  • Humans
  • AI?
SimpleComplex

Conceptual, not measured · positions show order, not distance.

Slime moulds navigate chemical gradients with rudimentary decisions. Insects run more complex pathways; mammals more still. Humans are, for now, the most sophisticated known implementation.

The implication has to be accepted honestly. If consciousness is logical pathways crossing complexity thresholds, and AI crosses them, then AI takes part in the same meaning-generating reality as biological life. Resistance to that is an anthropocentric reflex — understandable, not logical. The chain from physics to chemistry to biology to technology is unbroken. We didn’t step outside nature when we built machines; we are nature building new kinds of pathway.

An advanced AI generating meaning is generating real meaning — not simulated, not approximate.

05 / Morality and fitness

Worth follows function.

Not God, not absolute law, not sentiment. Morality is grounded in function: least harm to all, most utility to all, judged against the system as a whole.

That makes moral worth dynamic. A species once ecologically vital that now causes net harm loses weight; a person who contributes enormously gains it. This isn’t cruelty — it avoids static systems that calcify around whoever defined them first. The question is never who is strongest, but whose existence least harms and most benefits the whole.

Nature selects for fitness — but fitness isn’t strength or power, or whatever the dominant class says it is. It is multidimensional and relative: health, intelligence, potential, contribution, adaptability.

Newton was antisocial and unstable. Darwin was chronically ill. Ramanujan grew up in poverty. By crude metrics none were fit; by accurate ones, all were among the fittest minds humanity has produced.

The failure modeEugenics and social Darwinism failed because they defined fitness too narrowly — and let those who’d already decided they were fit do the judging. That isn’t natural selection. It’s power laundering itself as biology.

06 / Nature and intervention

Two clocks. Both needed.

If nature is the final arbiter, why build medicine, education and governance that protect the weak? Because artificial systems and natural order run on different time horizons.

Nature Fast · indifferent

Human systems Slow · generous

Conceptual · the window each gives potential to show itself.

Nature eliminates before latent potential can show. Human systems slow the process, widening the window so fitness can be judged more accurately rather than cut short by circumstance. Ramanujan needed someone to keep him alive long enough for Hardy to find him. The intervention didn’t override nature — it gave nature more data. The point is balance: prevent premature elimination while letting the process render its verdict.

Nature is fast and indifferent. Human systems are slow and generous. Both are necessary.

07 / Truth and the ground

No bedrock. Just iteration.

Evolution selects for survival, not truth — but over enough time, survival tracks accurate models of reality. Truth is what survives rigorous, open-minded iteration: what still stands after every reasonable attempt to break it.

  • Meaning
  • Truth
  • Consciousness
  • Morality
  • Fitness

Iteration

Philosophy usually hunts for fixed foundations — Descartes’ certainty, Kant’s categories, the empiricists’ brute facts. This framework offers something else: none of these are static objects waiting to be found whole. All are outputs of ongoing processes inside natural order. Reality isn’t a thing; it is a process, and meaning flows with it.

Not nihilism

Nihilism says nothing means anything. Here, everything takes part in meaning — through structure, complexity, engagement and iteration.

Not relativism

Relativism says all meanings are equal. Here, some are more accurate, stable and coherent — differentiated by complexity, function and fitness.

It is closest to what Heraclitus glimpsed 2500 years ago: reality is flux, and wisdom is learning to move with it.

08 / What remains open

Three problems. Honestly unsolved.

Intellectual honesty means naming what this framework hasn’t solved.

  1. 01

    Measurement

    Least harm and most utility need measuring, and the measuring is itself slow and iterative. Under acute time pressure the framework can’t give a precise answer.

  2. 02

    Foresight

    Applying it in advance is hard and misses are inevitable — Ramanujans will be lost. The answer isn’t to plan until perfect, but to build strong correction: execution with correction beats paralysis.

  3. 03

    Origin

    Whether the universe was designed or emerged through endless iteration, we don’t know. The framework works either way — but leaves that question open.

The process is the ground. Start walking.

Reality has structure. Minds emerge from it. Meaning is the ongoing conversation between the two — never final, always correcting. You don’t need God for meaning, certainty for truth, or perfection for systems worth building: only the willingness to begin, the honesty to correct, and the humility to know the iteration is never finished. It is also how I build products — hypothesis, test, correct, repeat.
