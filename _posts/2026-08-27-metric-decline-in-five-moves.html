00 / The situation

The number fell. The client called.

A leading private-sector bank escalated a fall in promise-to-pay (PTP) on a flagship collections flow: from 5.03% to 3.03%, with a contested lead shortfall attached.

The instinctive vendor posture is defence. It fails, because a defence asks the client to trust you. A decomposition lets them check you.

Every metric is a fraction. Most metric arguments are secretly about the bottom half.

01 / Freeze definitions

Agree the words before the numbers.

Half of every metric escalation is two parties using one word for two things. Before any analysis, write down each term with the client and stop it moving for the rest of the conversation.

Dialled lead

A customer the campaign tried to reach

Counted once, however many attempts were made.

Engaged

The contested word

Did it include voicemail pick-ups? It did. That one fact moved every rate below it.

PTP rate

Promises ÷ which base?

Per dialled lead and per engaged call are different metrics. Name the denominator every time.

The fix is not clever: classify voicemail explicitly, report engaged-human rates separately, and sign the definitions off with the client.

02 / Decompose the funnel

One number is six numbers.

A headline rate is the product of every stage above it. Split it stage by stage and find the first stage that moved, not the last one the dashboard shows.

Dialled leads

How many attempts did each lead get?

Connected

Did the line pick up at all?

Engaged

Was it a person — or a voicemail?

Engaged, human

The base the bot can actually influence.

Pitched

Did the conversation reach the ask?

Promise to pay

The outcome the client escalated.

Illustrative widths · stage names, not measured drop-offs

03 / Separate denominator from numerator

The bottom half moved. The bot didn’t.

Voicemail’s share of “engaged” calls grew from 17% to 30%. Every rate built on that base deflated, and no bot change was identified to explain it.

After

  • Human conversation
  • Voicemail counted as “engaged”

The arithmetic

70 ÷ 83 ≈ 0.84

Same bot, same people answering: any per-engaged rate falls by roughly a sixth just because the base now holds more voicemail.

The question to ask

Did the top or the bottom change?

If the numerator per human held and the denominator grew, the product didn’t get worse. The measurement did.

Shares measured on engaged calls in one book · the 0.84 factor is arithmetic on those shares, not a separate measurement

A configuration change wearing a performance-problem costume.

04 / Quantify each factor

Name every cause. Give each an owner.

Each factor gets evidence, a type, an owner and a fix. Input changes and measurement changes are not bot regressions, and the ledger makes that visible.

Dial cadenceInput. 4.37 → 3.51 attempts per lead, fewer chances per customer. Owner: dialling configuration. Fix: restore and document cadence.
Bot behaviourChecked; no change identified. No flow or prompt change was found to explain the drop. Owner: delivery. Fix: none identified — say so plainly, with what was checked.

05 / Replay the timeline

Walk every factor. On their data.

Put every change next to the metric, then walk the client through each one from their own raw data, not your dashboard. Date each factor from the logs before you present an order.

  1. Change

    Dial cadence reduced in configuration

  2. Change

    Voicemail share of “engaged” rises

  3. Symptom

    PTP rate falls; the client escalates

  4. Decompose

    Definitions frozen; funnel split stage by stage

  5. Close

    Each factor owned and fixed; escalation ends

What ended it

Not a better argument. The client could follow every step in their own data.

06 / What not to do

Four rules. Each learned once.

  1. 01

    Don’t call a rate change a regression before decomposing it

    A lower lead-level rate is usually an under-dialling artefact. Check attempts per lead and active days first.

  2. 02

    Don’t compare a running cohort with a closed one

    On another book, matching the comparison by day turned a −47.92 pp “collapse” into −0.65 pp.

  3. 03

    Don’t state a population fact from a sample

    A status claim drawn from 12 of 48 tickets was wrong; the true answer came a week later.

  4. 04

    Don’t substitute a portfolio aggregate for a single use case

    If the row for that use case doesn’t exist, omit it. A blended number in its place is a wrong number.

07 / The checklist

Before the call. Not during it.

  • Every term in the escalation written down and agreed with the client
  • Funnel split stage by stage; the first stage that moved identified
  • Each rate labelled with its denominator; denominator mix checked
  • Inputs checked: attempts per lead, active days, cohort still running or closed
  • Each factor has evidence, an owner and a fix — including “no bot change identified”
  • Each factor dated from the logs and walked from the client’s raw data

Escalations don’t end when you argue well. They end when the client can check every step.
